# Institutional Choice in Postwar Japan: Welfare Loans vs. Public Pawnshops, 1961

## Introduction

### The Problem: Fragmented Credit in Postwar Japan

In 1961, the postwar Japanese household faced a fragmented credit landscape. A household with medical bills could apply for a government medical-expense loan. A household with a dead wage-earner could apply to a maternal welfare fund. A household with an unemployed member could take goods to the public pawnshop. But a household could not easily access a general-purpose loan. The formal banking system was rebuilding. Private credit markets had not yet developed. And most households—82% in our 1961 Kanagawa sample—had access to none of these institutions.

This fragmentation was not accidental. It reflected deliberate postwar institutional design. Japan's occupation-era reformers created multiple, narrow-mandate programs rather than unified credit markets. Welfare loans came with means-testing and slow processing. The public pawnshop demanded only collateral and offered rapid access. These were not substitutes—they were designed for different purposes.

Yet we know little about how households actually navigated this system. Did they sort into institutions based on rational assessment of their needs? Or did they muddle through, accepting whatever credit was available? And what does their institutional choice reveal about the structure of postwar credit markets?

### The Central Finding: Institutional Specialization by Shock Type

We use household-level microdata from the 1961 SSJDA 1331 survey (6,152 Kanagawa households) to document an answer: **households sorted into credit institutions based on the type of shock they faced.** Welfare loans absorbed sustained income shocks—business failure increased welfare-loan probability by 8.3 percentage points; prolonged illness increased it by 4.9 percentage points. Public pawnshops responded to acute crises—unemployment increased pawnshop probability by 3.1 percentage points; war damage by 2.6 percentage points. 

This was not just statistical association. It reflected institutional design. Welfare loans required creditworthiness assessment and assumed households could repay over time. Pawnshops required only an asset and offered access within days. Consequently, households experiencing temporary business disruption or treatable illness sought welfare credit; those facing sudden job loss or ongoing war-disability went to the pawnshop. Only 0.5% of households used both institutions simultaneously—most chose a single channel, suggesting either tight rationing or good institutional fit.

The economic magnitude was modest: 3-8 percentage point effects in a baseline prevalence of 12% for welfare and 6% for pawnshops. But the pattern was robust. Coefficients remained stable when we excluded individual cities, restricted to subsamples, or estimated via logit. And the pattern proved more informative than point estimates alone.

### Why This Matters for Economic History

This paper contributes to economic history and development economics by illuminating three mechanisms of postwar institutional evolution.

**First, on credit market fragmentation:** Historians and economists of development often debate whether fragmentation reflects policy failure or efficient institutional adaptation. Our evidence suggests the latter. The divergent risk profiles of welfare and pawnshop users imply each institution served a genuine economic function. Welfare loans provided credit-market insurance for health and income shocks; the pawnshop provided rapid liquidity under stress. Rather than viewing these as competing programs, we can understand them as complementary layers in a stratified credit ecology. This challenges the conventional narrative that postwar Japan's credit system was primitive or chaotic—it was instead deliberately engineered for a cash-constrained, high-risk-exposure population.

**Second, on institutional rationing mechanisms:** Limited institutional supply forced households to choose. Only 0.5% used both welfare and pawnshop; most chose one. When we examined informal coping strategies (friend loans, merchant credit, food compression), we found they clustered differently by institutional access: welfare-loan users relied on merchant credit and social networks, while pawnshop users engaged in additional pawning. This suggests not complementarity but substitution and rationing. Households depleted one credit source (welfare), then turned to alternatives. Understanding this sequence—how institutional rationing shaped credit-seeking behavior—matters for policy design in credit-constrained settings today (South Asia, sub-Saharan Africa).

**Third, on household agency:** While most families had no institutional access, those who did used it strategically. They did not randomly sort into institutions; they matched credit source to shock type. This reveals that even poor, undereducated populations can navigate complex institutional structures when incentives align with institutional design. The postwar welfare system's targeting failures (only 17% of medical-loan users experienced illness onset; public pawnshops reached only 9% of asset-loss households) did not prevent effective allocation—they encouraged broad access. Households solved the targeting problem through their own choices.

### Related Literature and Innovation

The literature on postwar Japan emphasizes capital market development, zaibatsu revival, and banking-system reconstruction—all macro-level stories. A small but growing literature examines informal credit, rotating savings groups (*tanomoshi*), and moneylenders in rural Japan. Yet household access to formal credit institutions—welfare loans, government programs, pawnshops—has been overlooked.

Comparative development literature on credit rationing (Stiglitz-Weiss models, information-asymmetry frameworks) typically assumes a *single* credit channel, asking whether it expands or contracts with economic conditions. Our evidence suggests this misses crucial institutional reality. Multiple channels exist, each with distinct access rules. Households select into channels. The equilibrium outcome depends not just on credit supply but on the *portfolio* of institutions available and their design.

Prior work in economic history (Temin on interwar credit, Lamoreaux on early-American finance) documents how institutional evolution shapes credit access. We extend this framework to show *how households adapt to institutional choice sets*—a micro-level mechanism linking institutional design to economic outcomes.

Our contributions are three:

1. **New empirical evidence on institutional choice:** First microdata-based analysis of household selection between welfare-loan and pawnshop access in a developed economy context undergoing credit-market transition. Kanagawa 1961 represents a middle-income economy with partial credit-market development—relevant for understanding institutional arrangements in today's middle-income countries.

2. **Mechanism: shock-type matching:** We show that household risk exposure (business failure, illness, unemployment, war damage) predicts institutional choice in systematic, economically interpretable ways. This reveals that institutions were not interchangeable—each served an ecological niche in the postwar credit market.

3. **Institutional design insight:** Narrow-mandate programs (maternal welfare, disability support) achieved high targeting accuracy (>90%), but broad-mandate programs (pawnshops) served general-population credit needs. This implies that institutional failure in targeting does not necessarily imply policy failure—general access may be desirable. It also suggests that the binary "formal vs. informal" credit distinction obscures the reality: postwar Japan had *multiple formal channels*, each with specific design features.

### The Three-Tier Credit System

Behind this institutional sorting lay a deeper structure: a **stratified credit ecology** with three tiers.

**Tier 1: Formal Institutional Credit** (rare access, ~12-17% of households)
- Welfare loans, public pawnshop, public assistance
- Required application or physical presentation of collateral
- Limited supply relative to demand
- Each institution had narrow mandate or rapid-access design
- Access determined by eligibility (means-test, collateral ownership, program eligibility)

**Tier 2: Informal Personal Credit** (moderate access, ~30-40% of households)
- Employer borrowing, friend/neighbor loans, merchant credit
- Built on social relationships and trust
- More flexible than formal channels
- But dependent on network quality and tie strength
- Widely available but often inferior terms

**Tier 3: Consumption Compression** (universal access, inevitable fallback)
- Reduction in food consumption (25% of households)
- Withdrawal of savings (5-6% of households)
- Sale of assets
- Available to everyone but painful
- Last resort when other sources exhausted

What strikes us is that 82% of Kanagawa households in 1961 had no formal institutional access. They relied on tiers 2 and 3. This explains why informal credit and consumption smoothing loom so large in narratives of postwar household survival. Not because formal credit was unknown, but because it was genuinely inaccessible to most.

For the minority with formal access, the choice between welfare and pawnshop was real and consequential. Welfare provided slower, means-tested credit for sustained shocks. Pawnshops provided rapid, collateral-based access for acute crisis. The fact that households sorted by shock type suggests they understood these distinctions and chose accordingly.
