# Chapter 2: Historical Background — Postwar Institutional Credit in Japan, 1945-1961

## 2.1 The Postwar Context: Institutional Innovation Under Credit Constraint

In 1961 postwar Japan, formal capital markets were rebuilding. The banking system had survived wartime destruction but was tightly regulated and primarily served large firms and government. Private credit markets had not yet developed. Most households—particularly in working-class and farming communities—had access to none of these formal channels.

Against this backdrop, occupation-era reformers and postwar policymakers designed multiple, narrowly-targeted welfare credit programs. These were not expressions of coherent welfare policy but rather pragmatic responses to specific crises: war-widowed households without income support, disabled war veterans requiring medical care, families unable to afford children's education, medical patients facing catastrophic illness costs.

Each program was designed with explicit targeting criteria and distinct access mechanisms. Maternal welfare funds reached mothers in fatherless households. Disability medical-care programs targeted disabled household members. Medical-expense loans served households experiencing health crisis. Special educational funds supported poor families with school-age children. Each assumed a specific need; each operated as a narrow-mandate program.

Alongside these welfare programs, Japan maintained public pawnshops (*shichiya*), which had existed for centuries but were formalized under government operation in the postwar period. Unlike welfare loans, pawnshops operated on collateral-based principles: a household brought an asset (clothing, furniture, tools, precious metals), received rapid cash, and could redeem the item by repaying the loan plus interest. No means-testing. No eligibility determination. No bureaucratic delay.

This created a **dual institutional structure**: welfare loans offering means-tested, slow credit for approved needs; public pawnshops offering rapid, collateral-based credit to anyone with an asset. Together, they represented the formal credit ecosystem available to ordinary households in 1961.

## 2.2 Institutional Specialization: Design, Function, and Access Barriers

### Welfare Loans: Designed for Sustained Needs

Welfare loans carried several distinctive features:

1. **Eligibility determination:** Applicants had to demonstrate need through means-testing, provide references, and satisfy program-specific criteria (e.g., maternal welfare required proof of fatherlessness and dependent children; medical-expense loans required medical documentation).

2. **Processing delay:** Applications involved multiple steps, interviews, and verification. Processing took weeks or months.

3. **Assumed creditworthiness:** Loans were extended assuming households could repay over time. Implicit credit assessment determined who received approval and loan amount.

4. **Program-specific purposes:** Each welfare fund had a narrow mandate. Households had to match their need to the appropriate program. A household experiencing multiple shocks might need to apply to multiple programs.

5. **Limited supply:** Welfare budgets were constrained. Not every eligible household received approval. Rationing was common.

The logic was clear: welfare loans provided credit-market insurance for identified risks (illness, fatherlessness, disability, educational needs). They assumed the shock was temporary and the household had baseline economic capacity. They required bureaucratic capacity and employed means-testing to target limited resources.

### Public Pawnshops: Designed for Acute Access

By contrast, pawnshops operated on opposite principles:

1. **No eligibility determination:** Any household with an asset could access credit. No means-test. No application. No waiting.

2. **Immediate processing:** A household brought goods to the pawnshop, negotiated price, received cash within hours.

3. **Collateral-based credit:** No credit assessment of the household. Loans were secured by the asset itself. Repayment interest reflected risk of non-redemption, not borrower risk.

4. **No program-specific purposes:** The pawnshop did not ask why a household needed credit. Food shortage, medical emergency, unemployment, business failure—all faced the same rapid access mechanism.

5. **Unlimited supply (within storage capacity):** As long as households brought assets, the pawnshop could extend credit. No rationing at the access stage.

The logic was equally clear: pawnshops provided rapid emergency liquidity when households faced immediate crises. They required minimal bureaucracy and made no judgments about need or deservingness. They served as pressure valves for households exhausted other credit sources.

### The Divergence

This institutional dualism created systematic divergence in who accessed credit and under what conditions:

- **Welfare loans** appealed to households experiencing *sustained* shocks requiring ongoing support: chronic illness, business disruption, educational needs, disability. These households benefited from assured, extended credit.

- **Pawnshops** appealed to households experiencing *acute* shocks requiring immediate liquidity: sudden unemployment, unexpected medical cost, crop failure, death of wage-earner. These households valued speed and unconditional access over favorable terms.

Neither institution was "better." They served different economic functions. Household institutional choice—welfare vs. pawnshop—reflected their specific crisis and how urgently they needed access.

## 2.3 Credit Market Fragmentation and Household Strategies

The fragmented postwar credit landscape forced households to navigate multiple institutions simultaneously. We identify a **three-tier credit ecology**:

### Tier 1: Formal Institutional Credit (Rare Access)

Welfare loans, public pawnshop, public assistance. All three required some application or collateral presentation. All three had limited supply relative to demand. Access was determined by eligibility (means-test, collateral ownership, program-specific criteria).

Among our 6,152 Kanagawa households in 1961:
- 12.3% had access to any welfare loan
- 6.1% had access to public pawnshop
- 0.5% had access to both

### Tier 2: Informal Personal Credit (Moderate Access)

Employer borrowing, friend/neighbor loans, merchant credit ("purchases on account"). These operated on social relationships and trust. More flexible than formal channels, but dependent on network quality and tie strength. Among Kanagawa households:
- 20% borrowed from friends/neighbors
- 12% received credit from employers
- 5% made purchases on account

### Tier 3: Consumption Compression (Universal Fallback)

Food reduction, savings withdrawal, asset sales. Available to everyone but painful. The last resort when other sources exhausted. Among Kanagawa households:
- 25.5% reported reducing food consumption when living expenses insufficient
- 6% reported withdrawing savings
- 3% reported selling assets

## 2.4 Household Navigation of Institutional Constraints

What distinguishes the postwar period is that **most households had no formal institutional access**. In our data, 82% of Kanagawa households had access to neither welfare loans nor public pawnshops. They relied entirely on tiers 2 and 3.

This helps explain why informal credit and consumption smoothing loom so large in narratives of postwar household survival. Not because formal credit was ideologically privileged or "better," but because it was structurally inaccessible to most.

For the minority with formal access, choices were consequential. A household experiencing business disruption could apply for a welfare loan (requiring patience and creditworthiness assessment) or take goods to the pawnshop (requiring an asset but offering rapid access). Different institutions suited different shocks.

The data shows that households made this match explicitly. Risk exposure predicted institutional choice in systematic ways:

- **Business failure** increased probability of welfare-loan use (business need systematic credit) and pawnshop use (business failure creates immediate liquidity need)
- **Prolonged illness** increased welfare-loan probability (chronic condition requiring sustained support) but not pawnshop probability
- **Unemployment** increased pawnshop probability (immediate cash need) more than welfare-loan probability
- **War damage** (residual from conflict-affected regions) increased pawnshop probability (ongoing financial shock, collateral-based access)

This sorting pattern—by shock type, by access speed, by creditworthiness requirement—reveals that institutional design shaped household outcomes. It was not random allocation. It was structured rationing and strategic choice under constraint.

## 2.5 Institutional Design and Targeting

Narrow-mandate programs achieved high targeting accuracy. Maternal welfare funds, for instance, reached 92% of female-headed households (their target population). Disability medical-care programs reached households with disabled members at high rates.

But broad-mandate institutions evolved beyond original design. Public pawnshops, intended as general emergency credit, served general-population liquidity needs: only 9% of pawnshop users reported asset loss (a specific crisis), suggesting pawnshops functioned as general-purpose credit rather than insurance against asset shocks.

Similarly, public assistance (q04_1), designed for extreme poverty, showed complex targeting patterns. Some households used it as standalone support; others combined it with welfare loans. Receipt of public assistance reduced probability of welfare-loan use (suggesting substitution), but did not eliminate it (suggesting complementarity for some households).

This complexity—narrow programs achieving precision, broad programs serving general needs—is not a policy failure. Rather, it reveals institutional evolution responding to demand. Programs designed for specific crises expanded to meet broader credit hunger. Households exploited these expansions.

## 2.6 Implications for Understanding Postwar Institutional Development

Three implications emerge from this institutional landscape:

**First, institutional fragmentation was adaptive, not chaotic.** The postwar credit system was not a poor reflection of an ideal unified system. Rather, multiple institutions—each with distinct design—addressed different household needs. Welfare loans provided credit-market insurance; pawnshops provided emergency liquidity. This differentiation was efficient given the constraints: limited government budgets, high household risk exposure, and underdeveloped private credit markets.

**Second, household agency operated within institutional constraints.** Families navigated the system strategically, matching credit source to shock type. But their choices were structured by institutional supply. Few households could simultaneously access welfare and pawnshop credit (0.5% in our data)—not because they didn't want to, but because supply was rationed. This suggests that institutional design—access rules, supply constraints, program mandates—directly shaped economic outcomes.

**Third, the binary "formal vs. informal" distinction obscures postwar reality.** Japan had multiple formal channels, each with specific design features (access requirements, processing speed, collateral vs. creditworthiness basis). Households did not choose "formal" or "informal" in the abstract. They chose specific institutions with specific characteristics, trading off speed vs. access cost vs. repayment terms. Understanding these micro-level institutional choices illuminates how fragmented credit markets function in practice.

## 2.7 Summary

By 1961, postwar Japan had constructed a multi-layered credit system: welfare loans for sustained needs, pawnshops for acute crisis, informal networks for regular income-smoothing, and consumption compression as universal fallback. Most households had no formal access and relied on layers 2 and 3. For those with formal access, institutional choice was real and consequential, reflecting both household characteristics (risk exposure, creditworthiness) and shock type (sustained vs. acute, insurable vs. uninsurable).

This institutional structure did not emerge from centralized plan but from pragmatic policy responses to postwar crises, combined with household strategic navigation of available options. Understanding how households selected institutions—what characteristics predicted welfare vs. pawnshop use, how rare simultaneous access was, what coping strategies complemented each institution—reveals how credit allocation functioned in a fragmented market. This is the subject to which we now turn.
