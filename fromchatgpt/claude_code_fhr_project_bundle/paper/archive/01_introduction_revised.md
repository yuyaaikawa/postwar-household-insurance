# Introduction

## The Transformation of Low-Income Household Finance

How did the formation of the welfare state transform the provision of credit to low-income households? Before the development of modern social protection, households with low and unstable incomes relied on a diverse set of institutions to cope with illness, unemployment, and income loss. Economic historians have shown that households stabilized consumption through precautionary savings, adjustments in family labor supply, purchases on account, borrowing from employers and neighbors, and pawn credit. Working-class households in interwar Britain combined numerous saving and credit instruments, while Japanese factory-worker households used savings, borrowing, and family labor to protect essential consumption (Scott and Walker 2012; Ogasawara 2024). Pawnshops also played an important role in household risk coping: pawn lending increased during the influenza pandemic of 1918–20, indicating that pawn credit helped Japanese households respond to adverse health shocks (Inoue 2021). Taken together, this literature shows that the financial lives of low-income households cannot be understood through conventional bank credit alone.

Much less is known about how this financial ecology changed once the state began lending directly to low-income households. Japan provides an informative setting in which to study this transition. Before the Second World War, pawnshops supplied small loans to households excluded from bank credit. After the war, a new institution entered this market: publicly funded welfare lending. Established in 1955, the Household Rehabilitation Fund provided loans for self-employment, employment preparation, housing, education, and medical care to low-income households unable to borrow in ordinary financial markets. Existing research has reconstructed the institutional development of the program and examined the experiences of its borrowers, particularly recipients of medical loans. Because these studies focus primarily on households that actually received welfare loans, however, they cannot show how recipients differed from low-income households that continued to use pawnshops or used neither institution.

## The Research Question: Institutional Allocation, Not Displacement

This paper explores how low-income households were allocated across financial institutions when the welfare state entered the market for credit. Specifically, it asks whether welfare lending in postwar Japan displaced the older system of public pawnshops or instead added a new credit institution serving a distinct group of households. The central finding is that welfare loans were not distributed uniformly to the poorest households. They were concentrated among households with identifiable financing needs and with economic and material foundations that could make rehabilitation and repayment appear feasible to welfare officials. The formation of the welfare state therefore did not simply incorporate low-income households into formal finance. It created a new boundary of creditworthiness within the low-income population.

More broadly, this paper argues that institutional choice in postwar Japan reflected the **sorting of households by shock type and creditworthiness criteria**. Welfare loans were allocated to households whose problems appeared addressable through targeted credit—business failure, health crises, educational needs—and whose material position suggested capacity for repayment. Public pawnshops served households with immediate liquidity needs and available collateral, regardless of the underlying cause of income loss. This division of labor was not one of simple substitution but of complementary institutional functions operating under different assignment rules.

## Data and Scope

The analysis uses the 1961 *Kanagawa Survey of the Borderline Poor*. The restored dataset covers 6,152 low-income households in six cities and simultaneously records the causes of low income—including unemployment, business failure, declining earnings, illness, war damage, and disability—as well as histories of public assistance, four types of welfare loans (maternal welfare, household rehabilitation, educational, and medical), and public-pawnshop use. It also contains information on income, employment, household assets, and the methods households used when living expenses were insufficient. These features make it possible to compare welfare-loan users and public-pawnshop users within the same low-income population. The analysis focuses on the sorting of households across institutions rather than the causal effects of borrowing.¹

## Main Findings

The analysis yields three main findings.

**First, welfare lending targeted specific needs rather than poverty itself.** Even after controlling for income, location, household composition, and employment, business failure is associated with an 8.3-percentage-point higher probability of welfare-loan use, while prolonged illness is associated with a 4.9-percentage-point increase. Illness-onset, another health-related shock, shows a slightly smaller association (approximately 1.8 percentage points). The individual programs also correspond closely to their stated purposes: use of the Household Rehabilitation Fund is associated with self-employment and demand for business capital; educational lending with schooling needs; and medical lending with demand for treatment. Welfare lending therefore operated less as unrestricted support for ordinary living expenses than as purpose-specific credit tied to particular plans for household rehabilitation.

**Second, the clearest distinction between welfare lending and public pawnshops lay in the household's underlying economic and material position.** Among households that used either welfare loans or public pawnshops, but not both, a history of public assistance is associated with a 15.6-percentage-point lower probability of belonging to the welfare-loan group. Households recorded as having low livelihood capacity are 14.5 percentage points less likely to belong to that group, while households whose principal breadwinner was in poor health are 11.4 percentage points less likely. Conversely, each additional household asset is associated with an approximately 3.8-percentage-point higher probability of welfare-loan rather than pawnshop use. Because business failure is associated with both institutions—and shocks like unemployment and war damage more strongly predict pawnshop use—the division of labor cannot be reduced to a simple distinction between different shocks. Rather, welfare lending appears to have relied on the household's prospective capacity for rehabilitation, whereas public pawnshops relied on currently available physical collateral.

**Third, welfare lending did not fully replace older forms of borrowing. Instead, households layered multiple sources of credit.** Welfare-loan users were more likely to borrow from friends and neighbors. Households with a history of public-pawnshop use were more likely to report current pawning, employer borrowing, and neighborhood borrowing. In particular, previous public-pawnshop users were approximately 38 percentage points more likely to report pawning goods when living expenses were insufficient, while welfare-loan users were 10.3 percentage points more likely to pawn than non-users. Simultaneously, households with institutional access were more likely to engage in consumption compression (food reduction, savings withdrawal, asset sales). These associations are not causal, but they indicate that postwar financial inclusion did not take the form of a transition to a single institution. Instead, households continued to layer multiple sources of credit, suggesting a **stratified credit ecology** with three tiers: (1) formal institutional credit (welfare loans and pawnshops, accessed by ~12-17% of households); (2) informal personal credit (employer borrowing, friend/neighbor loans, merchant credit, accessed by ~30-40%); and (3) consumption compression (available universally as a last resort).

## Theoretical and Historical Contributions

This paper makes three contributions to economic history and development economics.

**First, it contributes to the economic history of household risk coping and alternative finance.** Studies of interwar Britain and Japan show that working-class households combined savings, family labor, and borrowing to stabilize consumption (Scott and Walker 2012; Ogasawara 2024). Research on pawn credit further demonstrates that pawnshops supplied credit to households excluded from banks and helped them respond to adverse shocks in Japan and Europe (McLaughlin 2013; Pastureau and Blancheton 2014; Inoue 2021). This literature, however, primarily examines the operation of individual financial institutions before the expansion of the welfare state. By comparing users of older collateral-based finance and newer welfare lending within the same low-income population, this paper shows that the state's entry into credit markets changed not only the quantity of credit available but also the criteria by which borrowers were sorted across institutions. Welfare lending created a new boundary of creditworthiness based on assessments of future household rehabilitation potential, not just current poverty or collateral availability.

**Second, the paper adds a historical perspective to the international literature on credit and welfare.** Existing research argues that credit access has served as both a substitute for and a complement to social policy, and that credit itself has been justified as an instrument of welfare provision (Trumbull 2012; Mertens 2017). Whereas this literature focuses largely on private consumer credit and mature welfare states in the later twentieth century, this paper examines a formative period in which the state itself became a lender to low-income households. The results show that the welfare state operated not only as a provider of transfers and insurance but also as a creditor that determined which poor households could be recognized as capable of repayment. In this respect, postwar Japan's welfare-lending programs embodied a new mode of governance: the state as arbiter of creditworthiness within the poor population.

**Third, this paper illuminates the institutional mechanisms of credit allocation under scarcity.** In economies where formal credit markets are fragmented and supply-constrained—a description fitting not only postwar Japan but also contemporary middle-income and low-income countries—multiple institutions coexist serving overlapping but distinct populations. The postwar Japanese case shows that institutional specialization (welfare loans for purpose-specific needs and demonstrated future capacity; pawnshops for rapid access and collateral-based lending) was adaptive, not chaotic. Each institution solved a different problem. Households matched their choices to institutional strengths while also layering institutions to manage compound risks. Understanding how households navigated this fragmented landscape—and how institutional design shaped the allocation of access—is crucial for contemporary policy debates about financial inclusion in credit-constrained settings.

## The Historical Transformation

The historical transformation documented in this paper was therefore not a simple transition from pawnshops to modern public finance. Welfare lending did not eliminate credit based on physical collateral. Instead, it added a new lending technology based on assessments of future household rehabilitation. Postwar financial modernization involved not the unification of credit institutions, but the hierarchical allocation of low-income households across institutions employing different standards of creditworthiness.

This allocation was not arbitrary. It reflected deliberate institutional design: welfare programs incorporated means-testing and eligibility criteria; they required documentation of need and proof of purpose-specific plans; they processed applications through bureaucratic review that assessed creditworthiness based on prospects for repayment. Pawnshops, by contrast, required only an asset and immediate negotiation of price. These design differences determined who accessed which institution. The poorest households—those with public-assistance histories, those recorded as having low livelihood capacity—faced barriers to welfare lending precisely because they could not credibly demonstrate capacity for the kind of household rehabilitation that welfare programs envisioned.
