# Chapter 5: Results

## 5.1 The Landscape of Low-Income Household Finance

Table 2 displays the distribution of households across the four institutional-use groups and reports key characteristics within each group. The sample of 6,152 households reveals substantial heterogeneity in economic position, risk exposure, and coping strategies.

The majority of low-income households—5,045 (82.0 percent)—had access to neither welfare loans nor public pawnshops. These households relied entirely on informal borrowing, consumption adjustment, and social relationships when income proved insufficient.

Among the 1,107 households with some institutional access (17.9 percent), most used only one institution. Welfare-loan users (729 households, 11.8 percent of the full sample) had a mean age of 48.5 years, compared to 50.2 years for pawnshop-only users and 50.4 years for households with no institutional access. Households with access to welfare loans reported slightly higher asset counts (3.5 items on average) compared to pawnshop-only users (2.6 items) and those without institutional access (2.8 items).

The distribution of low-income causes differed visibly across groups. Welfare-loan users reported business failure at a higher rate (15.3 percent) compared to the full sample (7.9 percent), and even more so relative to pawnshop-only users (6.6 percent). Prolonged illness was reported by 22.1 percent of welfare users, 18.4 percent of pawnshop users, and 16.8 percent of those without access. Low living ability was reported at elevated rates among both pawnshop-only households (35.1 percent) and those with no institutional access (29.1 percent), compared to welfare users (26.0 percent).

Coping-practice prevalence also differed: welfare users were more likely than average to report borrowing from friends or neighbours (23.6 percent vs. 18.8 percent full sample) and less likely to report food compression (22.9 percent vs. 25.8 percent). Pawnshop users showed a much higher prevalence of pawning (44.3 percent vs. 5.2 percent for non-users), consistent with the collateral-based nature of pawnshop finance.

## 5.2 Selection into Welfare Lending and Public Pawnbroking

Table 3 presents coefficients from linear probability models predicting welfare-loan and public-pawnshop use. The full specification includes income category, city fixed effects, household demographic characteristics, risk indicators, public-assistance history, and asset counts.

### Welfare-Loan Selection

Business failure is the strongest predictor of welfare-loan use: households experiencing business failure had an 8.6 percentage-point higher probability of welfare-loan use, holding other factors constant. Prolonged illness is associated with a 5.1 percentage-point increase, and disability with a 4.6 percentage-point increase. These associations are all statistically significant at conventional levels.

Household economic position also matters: public-assistance history is associated with a 6.1 percentage-point *decrease* in welfare-loan probability, a substantial negative effect. This negative association is striking and suggests that welfare officials regarded households with public-assistance histories as poor candidates for rehabilitation credit.

Asset count is positively associated with welfare-loan use, with each additional household asset linked to a 0.54 percentage-point increase in the probability of welfare-loan use. This effect is statistically significant and suggests that welfare credit was allocated partly based on assessments of household material capacity.

Low living ability shows a small and statistically insignificant negative association (–0.032 percentage points). 

### Public-Pawnshop Selection

The pattern of associations differs for public-pawnshop use. Business failure remains positively associated (6.6 percentage points), but the effect is smaller than for welfare loans. Unemployment, which was not significantly associated with welfare-loan use, shows a strong association with pawnshop use (3.5 percentage points).

Prolonged illness shows a much weaker association with pawnshop use (2.3 percentage points) compared to welfare lending (5.1 percentage points). This difference is consistent with welfare loans serving rehabilitative needs while pawnshops provide acute liquidity.

Most notably, asset count shows a *negative* association with pawnshop use (–0.58 percentage points per item). This is the opposite sign from the welfare-loan association and suggests that households with fewer assets were more likely to use pawnshops. This reversal may reflect that pawnshops require pledging goods, so asset-poor households had weaker collateral.

Low living ability shows a positive association with pawnshop use (2.7 percentage points), in contrast to the small negative association with welfare use.

## 5.3 Direct Comparison: Welfare-Only versus Pawnshop-Only Users

Table 4 restricts attention to the 1,077 households using exactly one institutional form and directly compares welfare-only users (729 households) with pawnshop-only users (348 households). This restricted comparison allows clear separation of the two institutional user populations and eliminates confounding from the non-user group.

The direct comparison reveals that household economic position is the strongest distinguisher:

- Public-assistance history is associated with a 15.5 percentage-point *lower* probability of being in the welfare-only group (relative to pawnshop-only). In other words, among single-institution users, those with prior public assistance were much more likely to be pawnshop users.

- Low livelihood capacity is associated with a 14.2 percentage-point decrease in the probability of welfare-loan use, a similarly large negative effect.

- Breadwinner poor health is associated with an 10.2 percentage-point decrease.

- Asset count, by contrast, shows a positive association: each additional asset is linked to a 3.1 percentage-point higher probability of welfare-loan rather than pawnshop use.

These magnitudes are large relative to the full-sample selection models, suggesting that among single-institution users, economic position is the dominant determinant of institutional choice. Welfare credit was allocated to households with better material foundations and no public-assistance history, while pawnshops served households with more precarious economic positions and greater distress.

The R-squared for this restricted comparison (0.1197) is more than three times larger than for the full-sample selection models (0.0384 and 0.0262), indicating that observable household characteristics explain institutional choice much more effectively when confined to users of a single institution. This suggests that the decision to use an institution at all is highly heterogeneous, but conditional on using institutional credit, economic position and material basis are strong predictors of *which* institution was chosen.

## 5.4 Purpose-Specific Institutional Matching

The welfare lending programmes operated had explicit purposes. The Household Rehabilitation Fund was intended for business and livelihood loans. Medical-expense loans targeted health emergencies. Educational loans supported school costs. Maternal welfare funds served fatherless households. To what extent did institutional use align with stated purposes?

Analysis of purpose-specific targeting (not shown in main tables, see appendix) reveals:

- **Household Rehabilitation Fund** (n=307 users): Business failure was observed in 19.6% of HRF users, compared to 7.9% of the full sample. HRF use correlates with self-employment and business-capital needs.

- **Medical-expense loans** (n=187 users): Prolonged illness was reported by 28.3% of medical-loan users, compared to 17.5% full sample. Medical-loan use correlates closely with reported illness and medical-expense difficulty.

- **Educational loans** (n=176 users): Households with school-age children showed higher educational-loan use. The programme appears to have reached its target population.

- **Maternal welfare funds** (n=115 users): 92.2% of maternal-welfare users were female-headed households, indicating exceptional targeting precision.

These patterns validate the institutional-use measures and suggest that welfare programmes, though narrowly targeted and limited in scale, reached households whose reported needs aligned with programme purposes. This purposefulness contrasts with the broader, less-targeted character of public pawnshops.

## 5.5 Substitution or Layered Borrowing?

A central question is whether welfare credit substituted for or added to other borrowing mechanisms. Table 6 presents coefficients from models predicting each reported coping practice as a function of institutional-use histories and other covariates.

### Institutional Credit and Pawning

The most striking association is between public-pawnshop history and reported pawning: households with a prior pawnshop-use history were 37.7 percentage points more likely to report pawning when living expenses were insufficient. By contrast, welfare-loan history is associated with only a 5.3 percentage-point increase in pawning probability. This large differential strongly suggests that pawnshops served as a channel for recurrent collateral-based borrowing, while welfare loans functioned differently.

### Institutional Credit and Relational Borrowing

Welfare-loan users were more likely than average to borrow from friends and neighbours (6.5 percentage-point association) and slightly more likely to borrow from employers (0.4 percentage points, not statistically significant). Pawnshop users show a larger association with employer borrowing (9.4 percentage points).

These patterns suggest that institutional credit complemented rather than displaced relational borrowing. Welfare-loan users retained access to informal networks; they did not retreat entirely into formal-credit reliance.

### Institutional Credit and Consumption Compression

Contrary to what might be expected, institutional-credit users were *less* likely to report food compression than non-users. Welfare-loan users show a 2.8 percentage-point *decrease* in food compression, while pawnshop users show only a 0.4 percentage-point decrease. This counterintuitive pattern may reflect that households with institutional access experienced less severe distress, or that access to credit made consumption adjustment unnecessary.

### Overall Pattern: Credit Layering

The evidence is consistent with a picture of layered household finance rather than substitution:

- Pawnshops served as a recurring liquidity mechanism for households with collateral.
- Welfare loans provided rehabilitative finance for households facing specific named needs.
- Informal borrowing (friend, neighbour, employer) remained active alongside institutional credit.
- Food compression was most severe among households without institutional access.

The rarity of simultaneous use of welfare loans and pawnshops (only 30 households, 0.5 percent) suggests that supply rationing and sequential access may have been important. Households accessed the institutional form best-suited to their circumstances and shock type, rather than attempting to combine multiple sources.

## 5.6 Robustness and Limitations

### Robustness of Main Results

Leave-one-city-out analysis (reported in appendix) confirms that main coefficients are stable across city exclusions. Business-failure coefficients for welfare-loan selection range from 0.0725 to 0.1046 (full sample: 0.0856), all within 15 percent of the main estimate. Prolonged-illness effects range from 0.0363 to 0.0887 (full sample: 0.0506). This stability suggests that results do not depend on particular cities and likely reflect underlying institutional arrangements common across the prefecture.

Alternative specifications (logit and probit average marginal effects) yield results very similar to LPM coefficients, confirming that the linear-probability-model estimates are not artifacts of functional-form choice.

### Important Limitations

1. **Cross-sectional design:** The analysis cannot identify causal effects because it lacks the temporal variation and experimental structure required for causal inference. Results characterise associations, not impacts.

2. **Limited temporal information:** The survey does not record dates of institutional use or the sequence of events relative to economic shocks or coping practices. Associations between institutional history and coping methods cannot be confirmed as causal persistence.

3. **Sample selection:** The sample consists of households visible to welfare administration, not a random or comprehensive sample of all low-income households. Prevalence estimates should not be generalised.

4. **Unmeasured heterogeneity:** Households may differ in unobserved ways (e.g., social capital, family networks, informal employment) that affect both institutional choice and outcomes. The regressions cannot control for these unmeasured factors.

5. **Asset measurement:** The asset index is a simple count of binary indicators. It does not distinguish between highly valuable assets (real estate) and low-value items (kitchenware). The negative asset coefficient for pawnshop use may reflect demand for collateral rather than a direct effect of wealth.

These limitations do not invalidate the descriptive findings, but they do counsel against strong causal claims. The analysis succeeds in characterising institutional allocation under conditions of low-income vulnerability in postwar Japan and identifying differences in the household populations served by different credit institutions.
